# Communication Guidelines — Technical Verbs

<EXTREMELY_IMPORTANT>

A technical verb is a verb from the subject's own vocabulary, outside the common vocabulary. The companion document covers technical names. The specimen documents carry the passage-length before-and-after pairs.

## Technical verbs

A word is a technical verb when it falls in one or more of these four categories. The listed words are examples, not a complete list.

1. **Manufacturing processes**, which give instructions to: remove material (drill, grind, mill, ream), add material (flame, insulate, remetal, retread), attach material (braze, crimp, rivet, solder, weld), change the mechanical strength, the structure, or physical properties of a material (anneal, cure, freeze, heat-treat, magnetize, normalize), change the surface finish of a material (buff, burnish, dress, passivate, plate, polish), or change the shape of a material (blend, cast, extrude, spin, stamp).
2. **Computer processes and applications**, for input/output processes (click, enter, press, print, swipe, tap, type), user interface and application processes (clear, close, copy, cut, delete, drag, open, paste, save, scroll, sort, zoom in), and system operations (abort, boot, debug, download, format, install, load, reboot, update, upload).
3. **Descriptions**, for descriptive texts only — general information, system description and operation, descriptive parts of service bulletins, technical reports, technical and legal papers. These cover mathematical, scientific, and engineering processes (bisect, compensate for, convert, detect, emit, modulate, radiate, transform), military processes (aim, arm, disable, explode, fire, intercept, load, lock on, parachute, unload), and regulatory language: waive (for inspection and requirements), comply with, conform to, supersede, meet (a requirement).
4. **Operational language**, for texts that tell users how to operate and use something correctly — a manual on how to use a phone, a tablet, a medical device, or a television set. Aircrew manuals and land or sea information sets are also operational texts. These verbs include: alert, approach, contact, descend, disembark, evacuate, fasten, fly, hover, inform, land, maintain, navigate, observe, respond, shut down, switch off, switch on, take off, verify.

A word is both a technical name and a technical verb when you can put it in a technical name category and also in a technical verb category. "Rivet" and "plate" are each a part and an action.

**MUST:**

1. You **MUST** hold technical verbs to the same rules as every other verb — the same forms, tenses, and voice.
2. You **MUST** use a common verb instead, whenever a common verb gives the instruction or the information accurately.

<EXAMPLE>

But you can write:
**Good:** The security scanner detects metallic objects.

</EXAMPLE>

3. You **MUST** use a specific technical verb, never one that is general and vague.

<EXAMPLE>

**Bad:** Machine the hole until it has a diameter of 8.00 +/- 0.003 mm.
**Good:** Ream the hole until it has a diameter of 8.00 +/- 0.003 mm.

</EXAMPLE>

4. You **MUST** leave a technical verb uncreated where none is necessary. If possible, use a simple common verb with an applicable technical name instead.

<EXAMPLE>

**Bad:** Grease the fasteners.
**Good:** Apply grease to the fasteners.

</EXAMPLE>

5. You **MUST** respect the context each category carries. Descriptive verbs belong in descriptive texts only and never in instructions. Operational verbs belong only in texts that tell users how to operate something. A word that is unavailable in general can still serve as a technical verb inside its own category.

<EXAMPLE>

**Good:** Enter your password. ("Enter" is a computer-process technical verb.)

</EXAMPLE>

**MUST NOT:**

1. You **MUST NOT** use a technical verb as a noun. You may use its past participle as an adjective.

<EXAMPLE>

**Good:** Lubricate the reamed hole.

</EXAMPLE>

</EXTREMELY_IMPORTANT>
