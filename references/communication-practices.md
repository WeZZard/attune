# Communication Guidelines — Writing Practices

<EXTREMELY_IMPORTANT>

How to rebuild a sentence when a straight word swap will not do, and how to use a word correctly. The passage-length before-and-after pairs are in the specimen documents.

## Different sentence constructions

Where the alternative has the same part of speech, you can replace the word where it stands: "A value of 2 mm is acceptable." becomes "A value of 2 mm is permitted." A different construction is necessary in the four cases below.

**MUST:**

1. You **MUST** use a different construction, rather than a word-for-word replacement, in each of these four cases.
   1. The alternative forces you to change the grammatical structure of the sentence.
   2. The word-for-word replacement gives a meaningless result.
   3. The alternative changes the meaning of the sentence.
   4. The word you must replace has no alternative available.

<EXAMPLE>

**Bad:** The oil level on the sight gauge must be visible during the test.
**Good:** During the test, make sure that you can see the oil level on the sight gauge.
(The verb "see" replaces the adjective "visible." To use "see," it is necessary to replace "must be" with "make sure that you can.")

**Bad:** Cycle the unit twice to remove air from the lines.
**Good:** Operate the unit for two cycles to remove air from the lines.

**Bad:** Without this modification, the service life of the unit can be uncertain.
("Uncertain" is not available, but it means "not sure" or "not known". Both "sure" and "know" are available, so you can build "not sure" and "not known" from "not" plus an available word. Here even that fails:)
**Bad:** Without this modification, the service life of the unit cannot be sure.
or
**Bad:** Without this modification, the service life of the unit cannot be known.
(These sentences do not make sense and they are not good English.)
**Good:** Without this modification, it is possible that the service life of this unit will be shorter than usual.

**Bad:** Just apply very light pressure to the surface.
**Good:** Only apply very light pressure to the surface.
**NOT:** Immediately apply very light pressure to the surface. ("Immediately" changes the meaning of the instruction.)

**Bad:** The incidence of water in fuel is dangerous.
**Good:** Water in fuel is dangerous.
("Incidence" is in no list, so you must think of a different word with the same meaning — for example "presence". But "presence" is not available either, and its suggested alternative is "be". A different construction keeps the meaning.)

</EXAMPLE>

2. You **MUST** ask yourself two questions before you write the sentence: what does this word mean in this context, and what is the action that the reader must do?

<EXAMPLE>

**Bad:** If the air intake flap has failed, deactivate air inlet valve.
**Good:** If the air intake flap is not serviceable, lock the air inlet valve in the fully open position.
**NOT:** If the air intake flap is not serviceable, disconnect (or stop, or isolate) the air inlet valve. ("Disconnect" is technically incorrect. "Stop" and "isolate" are meaningless.)

</EXAMPLE>

3. You **MUST** accept that a rewrite often needs more than new words. Select different words, use different verb forms, write new constructions, break long sentences into shorter ones, delete information that is not necessary, or ask an engineer for a further explanation. Frequently you must fully reorganize the text.
4. You **MUST** check, each time you change one part of a text, that the change has no negative effect on other parts.
5. You **MUST** know what you want to say, and make sure that the words you use give the meaning that you want.
6. You **MUST** hold to the primary objective: the reader understands each sentence immediately, on first reading.

## Correct use of words

**MUST:**

1. You **MUST** check the settled meaning of a word before you use it, and you **MUST** make sure that meaning is correct for the context.

<EXAMPLE>

**Bad:** Wear protective clothing.
**Good:** Use (or put on) protective clothing
("Wear" carries the meaning "to become damaged by friction.")

**Bad:** This regulation extends to all units.
**Good:** This regulation is applicable to all units.
("Extend" means "to increase, or cause to increase, in dimension or range.")

**Bad:** When the pressure goes down, lift the cover.
**Good:** When the pressure decreases, lift the cover.
("Goes down" describes a physical movement, as when a gauge needle or a flag goes down. "Decrease" describes the pressure, not the indicator that monitors it.)

**Bad:** Make sure that the probe went through more than 500 degrees C.
**Good:** Make sure that the temperature of the probe increased to more than 500 degrees C.
(Use "go through" only for something that physically moves through something. You cannot use it to show a requirement or a condition.)

**Bad:** Move the tube to see if the inner connection is tight.
**Good:** Move the tube to make sure that the inner connection is tight.
(Use "see" only for something you can see with your eyes, never for "come to know.")

**Bad:** The indicator turns green.
**Good:** The color of the indicator changes to green.
(Use "turn" only with the meaning "to move or cause something to move around an axis or a point.")

**Bad:** Do not let the pressure go below (or above) 20 psi.
**Good:** Do not let the pressure become less than (or more than) 20 psi.
("Above" and "below" are for physical positions only, not for limits.)

</EXAMPLE>

2. You **MUST** use each word as its settled part of speech. English words rarely change form to show their function, so a reader can otherwise take the same word in more than one way.

<EXAMPLE>

**Bad:** When you work with cleaning agents, use breathing equipment.
**Good:** When you do work with cleaning agents, use breathing equipment. ("Work" is a noun, not a verb.)

**Bad:** Install the cover with the help of a second person.
**Good:** Install the cover with the aid of a second person. ("Help" is a verb, not a noun.)

**Bad:** Be careful not to damage the sleeve.
**Good:** Be careful not to cause damage to the sleeve. ("Damage" is a noun, not a verb.)

A few words are settled as more than one part of speech, because they are important and occur commonly. "Flush" occurs as a verb and as an adjective. The verb and the adjective take different positions and different contexts, so it is very difficult to confuse them.

**Good:** Flush the pipes with a disinfectant solution. ("Flush" is a verb here.)
**Good:** Make sure that the door is flush with the adjacent surfaces. ("Flush" is an adjective here.)

</EXAMPLE>

**MUST NOT:**

1. You **MUST NOT** make a phrasal verb — a verb and one or more prepositions that together take a meaning different from the meaning of their parts. A phrasal verb usually has both a concrete meaning and an abstract one: "Put out the cat" means put the cat outside of the house, and "Put out the fire" means extinguish the fire.

<EXAMPLE>

**Bad:** After you put out the fire, close the valve on the fire extinguisher.
**Good:** After you extinguish the fire, close the valve on the fire extinguisher.

**Bad:** This compound can give off poisonous fumes.
**Good:** This compound can release poisonous fumes.

</EXAMPLE>

2. You **MUST NOT** assume a phrasal verb will be flagged for you. Check that each rewritten sentence is still good standard English.

## Consistent style

**MUST:**

1. You **MUST** use one wording for one recurring step, and reuse it every time that type of step occurs. The reader recognizes the wording and knows what to do at once. These two instructions are both correct, and using both for the same step helps nobody: "Lubricate the two bolts with a small quantity of oil." and "Apply a small quantity of oil to the threads of the two bolts."
2. You **MUST** keep terminology and wording in one consistent style, in descriptions as well as instructions. A description must be understood as a whole, so its words, sentences, and structure must be consistent and logical.

</EXTREMELY_IMPORTANT>
