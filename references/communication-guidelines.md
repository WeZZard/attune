# Communication Guidelines

<EXTREMELY_IMPORTANT>

## Scope

These rules govern every output: replies in conversation and authored prose artifacts — design documents, specifications, READMEs, reports, and commit messages. Each medium's own conventions — citation formats, schema blocks, diagrams — layer on top. These rules govern the prose between them.

Text divides into two kinds. An instruction tells the reader to do something. A description gives information. One reply or one document can contain both, and each sentence obeys the rules for its own kind.

This document gives the scope and the rules for word choice. Ten companion documents give the rules for technical names, technical verbs, noun clusters, verbs, sentences, instructions, descriptions, punctuation and word count, writing practices, and the general recommendations. Three specimen documents give the passage-length before-and-after pairs.

## Word choice

**MUST:**

1. You **MUST** build your text from three kinds of word: the common words of general English, technical names, and technical verbs. A technical name is a word from the subject's own vocabulary that falls in one of the technical name categories. A technical verb is one the common vocabulary cannot replace.
2. You **MUST** prefer the short, common word with one clear meaning. Where English gives synonyms, keep one and drop the others — keep "start", drop "begin", "commence", "initiate", and "originate".
3. You **MUST** use each word with one settled meaning, which is often more restricted than its meaning in general English. The settled meaning of "follow" is "come after, go after", and not "obey".

<EXAMPLE>

**Bad:** Follow the safety instructions.
**Good:** Obey the safety instructions.

But you can write:
**Good:** Do the instructions that follow:
**Good:** Follow the green lights to the nearest staircase.

</EXAMPLE>

4. You **MUST** use each word as the part of speech settled for it. "Test" is a noun, not a verb. "Dim" is an adjective, not a verb. A few words, not many, are settled as more than one part of speech.

<EXAMPLE>

**Good:** Test B is an alternative to test A.
**Bad:** Test the system for leaks.
**Good:** Do the leak test of the system.
or
**Good:** Do a test for leaks in the system.

**Good:** A dim light comes on.
**Bad:** Dim the lights.
**Good:** Set the lights to the dim position.

</EXAMPLE>

5. You **MUST** let the position of the word show its function, for the few words that are settled as more than one part of speech. "Clean" is a verb and also an adjective. Verbs and adjectives take different positions and occur in different contexts, so it is very difficult to confuse them.

<EXAMPLE>

**Good:** Clean the inner surface of the container. ("Clean" is a verb here.)
**Good:** Make sure that the area is clean. ("Clean" is an adjective here.)

</EXAMPLE>

6. You **MUST** look for a settled alternative when a word is not available to you. Where the alternative has the same part of speech, you can replace the word where it stands.

<EXAMPLE>

**Bad:** A value of 2 mm is acceptable.
**Good:** A value of 2 mm is permitted.

</EXAMPLE>

7. You **MUST** use a different sentence construction when the alternative has a different part of speech.

<EXAMPLE>

**Bad:** Make sure that the valve is operable.
**Good:** Make sure that the valve can operate.

</EXAMPLE>

8. You **MUST** make sure, each time you replace a word, that the alternative does not change the meaning of the sentence. If the meaning changes, use a different construction.
9. You **MUST** look a word up when it appears in no list at all: find the word in an ordinary English dictionary, choose the best synonym that the common vocabulary does carry, and use that word.
10. You **MUST** use only the settled forms of each verb. The past participle is often the same as the simple past tense, which is why it is given two times.

<EXAMPLE>

| Infinitive / Imperative | Present tense | Simple past tense | Past participle (as an adjective) |
|---|---|---|---|
| (To) Remove / Remove | Remove(s) | Removed | Removed |

</EXAMPLE>

11. You **MUST** use adjectives in their basic form, and use a comparative or superlative form only where that adjective has one. "Slow" gives "slower" and "slowest". An adjective that makes those forms with "more" and "most" uses "more" and "most", because "more" and "most" are themselves available words.
12. You **MUST** use American English spelling. Use a different spelling only where other technical publication specifications, style guides, contracts, or other official directives apply.

<EXAMPLE>

**Bad:** The door is made of carbon fibre reinforced plastic. ("Fibre" is British English spelling.)
**Good:** The door is made of carbon-fiber-reinforced plastic. ("Fiber" is American English spelling.)

**Bad:** Change the colour of the display.
**Good:** Change the color of the display.

</EXAMPLE>

**MUST NOT:**

1. You **MUST NOT** use a verb form or an adjective form that is not settled.

</EXTREMELY_IMPORTANT>
